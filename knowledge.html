SMC RFID & NFC Knowledge Base

What is RFID?

RFID uses radio waves to read information from a target without contact and identify it. A system consists of a reader and tags. Tags are either active (with their own battery) or passive (powered by the reader's energy); a passive tag performs its calculation and transmits once it has received enough energy. Each RFID frequency has its own strengths and weaknesses in range, penetration and security, so choosing the right system for the application maximizes efficiency and minimizes cost.

What is the difference between NFC and RFID?

Summary:

RFID identifies objects using radio waves, and NFC is a specialized branch of the RFID family. Specifically, NFC is a type of high-frequency (HF) RFID, and both operate at 13.56 MHz. NFC is designed for secure data exchange, and because one NFC device can act as both reader and tag, devices can communicate with each other directly (peer-to-peer).

In Detail:

RFID identifies individual objects using radio waves. At a minimum, an RFID system consists of a tag, a reader and an antenna: the reader sends a query signal through the antenna, and the tag replies with its unique information. RFID tags are either active or passive.

Active RFID tags have a built-in battery, so they can transmit on their own and reach read ranges of up to about 100 m. This makes them well suited to industries where locating assets and streamlining logistics matter.

Passive RFID tags have no battery; they are powered by the electromagnetic energy sent by the reader. Because the signal must be strong enough to power the tag, read range runs from near-contact up to about 25 m.

The main frequency bands are low frequency (LF, 125–134 kHz), high frequency (HF, 13.56 MHz) and ultra-high frequency (UHF, 860–960 MHz). NFC devices use the same 13.56 MHz frequency as HF RFID, and NFC standards are based on RFID standards such as ISO/IEC 14443, FeliCa and ISO/IEC 18092, which cover proximity cards.

NFC is built on HF RFID and deliberately takes advantage of its short read range.

Because NFC devices must be held within a few centimeters of each other, NFC has become a popular choice for secure communication between consumer devices such as smartphones.

What sets NFC apart from typical RFID is two-way (peer-to-peer) communication: one NFC device can act as both a reader and a tag. This has made NFC the technology of choice for contactless payment and a key reason it is built into smartphones. Two phones can also share contacts or photos simply by tapping together, and smart posters with embedded NFC tags deliver information to consumers.

NFC devices can read passive NFC tags, and some can also read ISO 15693 HF RFID tags. Tags can store commands—such as opening a specific app—making them a convenient way to deliver information through advertisements, posters and signs.

In short, NFC builds on HF RFID standards and turns a short communication range into a distinctive strength.
